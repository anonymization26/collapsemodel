\section{Introduction}
Researchers adapting a predictor to a target domain often have several source-data pools to choose from. These pools differ in acquisition conditions, image style, or example coverage. Comparing their combinations by training and validating a predictor on each incurs repeated work, even when each predictor is small. We study whether an earlier, feature-based comparison can produce a competitive final choice with fewer supervised evaluations. The objective is to reduce the work of choosing training data, rather than to identify the best-performing combination from geometry alone.

Pretrained encoders provide a common comparison space. They map images to feature vectors that can be reused across source combinations when encoder parameters stay fixed; CLIP and DINOv2 support this setting \citep{radford2021,oquab2024}. Before fitting a classifier on these features, called a readout, we can compare source coverage of feature directions and its relation to the target.

Broad coverage alone does not establish target relevance. Effective rank exponentiates the entropy of normalized singular values to summarize how evenly variation spans directions \citep{roy2007,garrido2023rankme}. A high value may reflect variation rarely encountered at prediction time, and individually diverse pools may overlap. Target features can distinguish relevant coverage from diversity that a source-only spectral summary treats alike.

Feature coverage does not determine the relation between features and labels. We therefore use geometry to retain alternatives and labeled validation to choose among them. Leaving good candidates available differs from selecting a good predictor: a screen is useful when the final loss is acceptable and obtaining that choice costs less.

We evaluate this two-stage data-pool screening protocol using target-weighted experimental design, which favors coverage that reduces uncertainty along target-relevant directions \citep{yu2006}, and second-moment matching, which compares source and target directional energy. Against target-blind diversity, we examine candidate omissions, validation choices, and the resulting loss across evaluation budgets and computation costs.

The experiments answer three parts of this question. On equal-sample-cost DomainNet combinations, short geometric lists give lower mean test Brier, a probability-prediction loss, than longer random lists: which combinations receive evaluation matters as well as how many. Independent timing then tests whether fewer evaluations pay for screening. They do with cached features; adding measured feature work preserves the logistic-regression saving but reverses it for ridge. Finally, comparisons with moment matching and domain-level controls examine what detailed geometry adds. Simple matching is competitive, and the usefulness of block information depends on construction and metric. Together, these results characterize the quality and cost of target-aware screening, rather than establish a single preferred ranking rule.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Optimal design and distribution matching}
A-optimal design measures uncertainty through the trace of a covariance matrix, with applications to active linear regression \citep{fontaine2021}. Transductive experimental design minimizes prediction variance over given target covariates \citep{yu2006}; its regularized linear objective is the target-weighted trace used here. We apply it to unions of fixed source pools, retaining several for labeled validation. MMD instead compares mean embeddings of source and target distributions \citep{gretton2012}; a homogeneous quadratic kernel makes this comparison depend on their second moments. Moment alignment also supports representation learning in multi-source domain adaptation \citep{peng2019}. With the encoder fixed, these criteria offer two interpretations of target relevance. Comparing them tests whether model-based uncertainty weighting contributes beyond direct distribution matching, not merely whether either beats target-blind diversity.

\subsection{Selection under a computation budget}
Compute-constrained data selection accounts for selection work and subsequent training \citep{yin2025}. Pool screening asks how many combinations should receive supervised evaluation. Broad shortlists may preserve candidates yet require substantial fitting. Measuring final quality, decision time, and feature preparation distinguishes fewer evaluations from a faster decision.

\subsection{Representation geometry and diversity}
RankMe uses effective rank to assess representations without downstream labels \citep{garrido2023rankme}. Determinantal point processes favor directionally complementary subsets \citep{kulesza2012}. These geometric objectives differ from preserving low-target-loss candidates: variation can be redundant for a task, whose important directions may occupy only part of the representation. We use merged effective rank and a specified subspace-DPP implementation as target-blind comparators.

\section{Geometry-Guided Screening}
\label{sec:problem}
\label{sec:theory}
\subsection{Problem and two-stage procedure}
A pool is a fixed block of examples; a candidate is a feasible union of blocks. A shortlist contains these combinations, not individual images. Screening and supervised evaluation produce one predictor, using separate target data for screening, validation, and testing.

Let $H_i\in\R^{n_i\times d}$ contain the features of pool $i$, using a fixed encoder and shared label-independent preprocessing. Its Gram matrix $G_i=H_i^\top H_i$ records feature-direction strengths and their relationships. For $S$ in the candidate family $\cC$, $G_S=\sum_{i\in S}G_i$ and $n_S=\sum_{i\in S}n_i$. Unlabeled target-selection features estimate the uncentered second moment $C_T=\E_T[hh^\top]$, which describes target directional energy.

The screen ranks $\cC$ and retains its first $s$ combinations as $\cQ_s$. Predictors trained on their source labels then compete on a separate target-validation set, which chooses $\widehat S\in\cQ_s$. Target-test data audit this frozen choice and candidate retention. Screening sees source and target-selection features but no labels or validation/test data; target-blind rules use only source information. The supervised stage reads source labels and target-validation data. These stages are recorded as U0 and U2. Geometry reduces the combinations that need fitting; labeled validation supplies the feature--label evidence needed to choose among them.

Each combination receives the same classifier and validation procedure; screening changes which combinations are evaluated, not the encoder. The equal-sample-cost study fixes training sample count, not elapsed time.

\subsection{Shared geometry and spectral diversity}
The singular value decomposition $H=U\Sigma V^\top$ separates feature directions from their strengths. For fixed nonnegative weights $w_i$, stacking the scaled source matrices gives
\begin{equation}
 G_S=H_S^\top H_S=\sum_{i\in S}w_iG_i.
 \label{eq:gram}
\end{equation}
The eigenvalues of this sum are the squared singular values of the pooled features. It preserves the strength and cross-pool alignment of directions, provided preprocessing coordinates remain fixed. The experiments use unit weights.

Effective rank normalizes the singular values into a probability vector and exponentiates its entropy \citep{roy2007}:
\begin{equation}
 \reff(H)=\exp\!\left(-\sum_j p_j\log p_j\right),\qquad
 p_j=\frac{\sigma_j(H)}{\sum_k\sigma_k(H)}.
 \label{eq:rank}
\end{equation}
For a nonzero matrix, $0\log0=0$ and $1\leq\reff(H)\leq\operatorname{rank}(H)$, with the upper endpoint attained by equal positive singular values. Two values in ratio $9:1$ give about $1.38$, versus two for equal strengths. Gram eigenvalues must be square-rooted before normalization; normalizing them directly defines another score. Repeating the entire matrix leaves effective rank unchanged: it measures spectral shape, not sample evidence. It is a target-blind comparator, not a step required by the target-aware scores.

Marginal spectra and principal angles alone need not determine even this pooled statistic: the same strengths can be assigned differently to shared and unshared directions. Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} gives a counterexample and conditions for a local spectral formula. Retaining Gram operators preserves these directions. Using the target moment is a separate step: it tells the score which preserved directions matter to the target.

\subsection{Target weighting and moment matching}
Target weighting asks which source combination reduces uncertainty where the target needs coverage. A shared linear model makes this question precise. Consider scalar Bayesian regression with $\theta\sim N(0,P_0)$, $P_0\succ0$, and $y=h^\top\theta+\epsilon$, where independent noise satisfies $\epsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$ and $\sigma^2>0$. Sources and target share $\theta$ and the noise model; target covariates reveal no further information about $\theta$. Conditional on the source design, the posterior covariance and posterior-mean target risk are
\begin{equation}
 P_S=\left(P_0^{-1}+\sigma^{-2}G_S\right)^{-1},\qquad
 R_T(S)=\sigma^2+\tr(C_TP_S).
 \label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}
The expectation averages posterior uncertainty and a new target observation. The derivation is recalled in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

Equation~\eqref{eq:risk} motivates the score $f_A(S)=\tr(\widehat C_TP_S)$, minimized with fixed hyperparameters. With an isotropic prior, this is the regularized target-prediction-variance criterion of transductive experimental design \citep{yu2006}; we retain the recorded name Target A-opt. Source observations reduce uncertainty along covered directions; the target moment weights the uncertainty by directional energy at prediction time. A pool can therefore be valuable without high overall diversity if it covers directions that remain uncertain and matter to the target. Adding a positive-semidefinite source Gram cannot increase this score.

For the experimental choice $P_0^{-1}=I$ and $\sigma^2=1$, write $G_S=U\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_j)U^\top$, including zero eigenvalues. Then
\begin{equation}
 f_A(S)=\sum_j\frac{u_j^\top\widehat C_Tu_j}{1+\lambda_j}.
 \label{eq:target-directions}
\end{equation}
The denominator reflects source coverage of $u_j$; the numerator measures its target importance. Combinations with identical eigenvalues can receive different scores when their directions align differently with the target. Effective rank treats their spectra identically; target weighting distinguishes where coverage is useful.

Second-moment matching asks a different question: how close is the source's average directional energy to the target's? It minimizes
\begin{equation}
 f_M(S)=\norm{G_S/n_S-\widehat C_T}_F^2.
 \label{eq:mmd}
\end{equation}
This is empirical squared MMD with kernel $k(h,h')=(h^\top h')^2$, including diagonal terms. It matches average directional energy rather than weighting posterior uncertainty. Both criteria read the same features, testing whether inverse-covariance weighting adds value beyond direct moment matching.

The shared conditional model supplies the risk interpretation of A-opt. Its assumption is substantive: if all features equal $1$, source pools labeled constantly $+1$ and $-1$ produce predictors whose risk ordering reverses between two target worlds with opposite constant labels. The feature geometry is unchanged. In classification, we therefore evaluate the scores using trained ridge-softmax and logistic-regression readouts rather than identify Equation~\eqref{eq:risk} with their losses.

\subsection{Separating retention from final selection}
The final choice can lose quality in two ways: screening discards a good candidate, or validation chooses a worse member of the retained set. Let $\ell(S)$ be target risk for a fixed trained predictor and $v(S)$ its validation loss. If $|v(S)-\ell(S)|\leq\varepsilon$ throughout $\cQ_s$, the validation minimizer satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \ell(\widehat S)-\min_{S\in\cC}\ell(S)
 \leq
 \underbrace{\min_{S\in\cQ_s}\ell(S)-\min_{S\in\cC}\ell(S)}_{\text{shortlist omission}}
 +2\varepsilon.
 \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
The bound, proved in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}, motivates two separate measurements. Candidate retention checks what screening leaves available; final test loss evaluates the choice made from that set. High recall need not give a good final choice if validation selects poorly. Conversely, a low-loss choice need not retain every top-ten candidate. We audit finite-test counterparts of omission and selection error alongside recall. The deviation condition explains their distinction; it is not estimated as a population guarantee.

\subsection{Decision budgets and cost accounting}
The full-search reference trains all candidates and chooses $S_{\mathrm{full}}=\arg\min_{S\in\cC}v(S)$. This is a deployable procedure. The test oracle, in contrast, is the candidate with lowest observed test loss and is used only for auditing. Let $b(S)$ be test Brier, the mean squared distance between predicted class probabilities and the one-hot label; lower is better. We report $b(\widehat S_s)-b(S_{\mathrm{full}})$ and the fraction of repeated tasks exceeding a declared absolute tolerance. The gap can be negative because the validation and test rankings can differ.

With frozen features available, screening costs ranking plus fitting and validating retained candidates. Starting from images adds encoder initialization and feature extraction; target-aware scores also need target-selection features. Screening pays off only if its overhead, including extra feature work, is smaller than the evaluations it replaces. Candidate counts are not elapsed time: the matrix operations used in linear scoring can also comprise a substantial part of fitting ridge readouts.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
The studies were conducted in stages. Synthetic tasks test the risk model; PACS and Office-Home \citep{li2017,venkateswara2017} evaluate direct selection against a prespecified practical-effect threshold, followed by a post-hoc headroom audit. The subsequent DomainNet shortlist configuration was frozen before feature extraction and outcome evaluation. Resampling, projection/readout changes, budget reanalysis, and repartitioning are exploratory follow-ups. Independent timing separately measures the cost of executing the selection procedures. Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} records configurations and result locations.

From the official cleaned DomainNet data \citep{peng2019}, we select 100 class names by a fixed hash rule. Each of six domains contributes disjoint sets of 1,536 target-selection images, 1,024 target-validation images, and 3,072 anchor-pool images from official training data, plus 2,048 official test images. The manifest contains 46,080 images. These roles separate information for screening, final selection, and independent evaluation. Class metadata define the benchmark vocabulary but are hidden from screening.

CLIP ViT-B/32 \citep{radford2021} serves as a frozen construction anchor. Within each domain, its first principal-component scores define low, middle, and high strata, each supplying a block of 512 images. For a given target, we exclude its own blocks and enumerate $\binom{15}{3}=455$ combinations, each with 1,536 source images. ResNet-50 and DINOv2-B/14 provide evaluation features \citep{he2016,oquab2024}. All rules share row normalization, a fixed Rademacher projection to 128 dimensions, and post-projection row normalization. Fixed sample count separates data quantity from combination quality; shared encoders and readout settings keep method comparisons from also changing the prediction model.

Target A-opt uses $P_0^{-1}=I$ and $\sigma^2=1$. Comparators are second-moment MMD, target energy $\tr(\widehat C_TG_S/n_S)$, Bayesian D-opt, merged effective rank, subspace DPP, domain balance, and 20 seeded random rankings. They score the same candidate family. Domain balance additionally reads source-domain identifiers. Kernel definitions and tie rules appear in Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}.

The screen produces nested shortlists of sizes $10,25,50,100,227$, with $227$ designated primary. In the original protocol, U2 independently evaluates each method's primary shortlist using ridge regression with regularization $1$, no intercept, and a fixed softmax temperature. Validation Brier selects the final combination, breaking ties lexicographically. Smaller lists reuse that method's nested evaluations; there is no cross-method cache. The test stage verifies upstream hashes before evaluating every combination to establish an exhaustive audit reference.

The follow-up adds four projection seeds and multinomial logistic regression with fixed regularization, preserving candidate members and data roles. Screen, validation, and test remain separate processes, but methods reuse each combination's fitted model for shortlist auditing. Its runtime measures a cached research audit, not separate deployments. Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} gives readout settings and provenance checks.

We measure how many test top-ten candidates survive screening and how far the validation choice lies from the test oracle $S^*$. Recall measures candidate retention, not classification accuracy; regret measures loss relative to the oracle:
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{Recall}_{10}(\cQ_s)=\frac{|\cQ_s\cap\mathrm{Top}_{10}(b)|}{10},\qquad
 \mathrm{Regret}(\widehat S)=\frac{b(\widehat S)-b(S^*)}{|b(S^*)|}.
 \label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
The original acceptance criteria require at least $50\%$ removal, mean recall of at least $90\%$, and mean normalized regret at most $5\%$. Brier is primary. Follow-up negative log-likelihood (NLL) and classification-error audits use their corresponding validation metric to select candidates, with the metric fixed before test access.

We average encoder results within each dataset--target-domain unit and random rankings within each task. PACS/Office-Home contain eight such units and DomainNet six. Follow-up seeds are also averaged within domains. Original bootstrap intervals resample target units; the test-sample analysis fixes trained models and resamples examples within each target. Seeds, encoders, and overlapping combinations are repeated measurements, not additional independent datasets.

\section{Empirical Findings}
\label{sec:results}
We first ask whether screening preserves final quality and saves computation on DomainNet. Retention then separates screening omissions from validation choices, before domain controls and sensitivity tests examine when the geometry is useful. The earlier shared-model and direct-selection studies explain the score's motivation and the distinction between improving predictions and reducing evaluation work.

For logistic regression, evaluating 50 A-opt or MMD candidates gives lower mean Brier than evaluating 227 random candidates. More random evaluations do not produce a better mean final choice in this comparison: which candidates receive evaluation matters. Both comparisons favor screening in five of six domain averages. Paired mean differences are $-0.001518$ for A-opt and $-0.001664$ for MMD; descriptive domain-bootstrap ranges are $[-0.003517,0.000023]$ and $[-0.003720,-0.000119]$. Shared source pools prevent interpreting these as independent-dataset confidence statements.

Task-level exceedance rates check whether favorable averages hide larger losses on individual tasks. At size 50, each geometric method exceeds an absolute Brier gap of $0.005$ to exhaustive validation in one of 48 repeated encoder--seed--domain tasks, or $2.08\%$. Random screening at size 227 exceeds it in $15.52\%$ after the prescribed averaging. Appendix Table~\ref{app:budget-table} reports final losses. The tolerance is part of an exploratory grid, not a new success gate; these post-hoc curves compare budgets but do not validate an automatic budget-selection rule.

\subsection{Candidate construction and source-domain controls}
\label{sec:construction}
Does a good shortlist require distinguishing blocks within source domains, or is domain composition enough? We compare mean matching and domain-level moment matching, with projection and readout parameters fixed, on the original blocks and a label- and feature-independent hash partition of the same images. Domain-level matching pools all blocks within each domain before scoring mixtures; it cannot distinguish candidates with identical domain composition. Appendix Table~\ref{app:construction-table} reports this exploratory challenge, separating randomized tie handling from the frozen lexicographic protocol.

Repartitioning preserves the broad Brier advantage over random screening. At size 50, domain-level matching and MMD give similar mean final Brier: domain-level moments suffice for a competitive global shortlist in this setting. Mean matching is competitive on the original blocks but less effective after repartitioning. These comparisons ask whether coarse information suffices overall, not whether blocks within a domain are interchangeable.

The within-composition comparison addresses the latter question. We group candidates containing the same number of blocks from each source domain and retain the floor of half of each non-singleton group. On the original blocks with logistic regression, A-opt and MMD retain the empirical Brier optimum in about $77\%$ and $76\%$ of groups, against $45\%$ for random selection. Repartitioning lowers these rates to about $56\%$ and $55\%$; both fall below random for classification error. These are within-group optimum-retention rates, not global top-ten recall. Useful block information can coexist with competitive domain-level matching, but its benefit depends on construction and metric. Neither comparison assigns a causal share of the overall benefit to domain composition.

\subsection{Projection and readout sensitivity}
\label{sec:readout}
The original ridge-softmax losses differ little: every candidate lies within the $5\%$ regret gate, so it does not distinguish screens. Two checks address this result. Four new projections test dependence on one coordinate compression; logistic regression tests retention when candidate losses are more separated. Source membership and sample cost stay fixed. Appendix Table~\ref{tab:readout} reports these exploratory new-seed averages, with the original seed as an implementation control.

A-opt retains $\ReadoutRidgeA\%$ of the Brier top ten on average under each readout, with MMD slightly higher. The mean relative best-to-worst Brier span increases from $\RidgeSpan\%$ under ridge-softmax to $\LogisticSpan\%$ under logistic regression. High recall thus persists in a setting with more separation between candidates, rather than appearing only when their Brier losses are almost equal. The two target-aware scores also retain most NLL- and error-ranked candidates on average, with MMD higher in these comparisons.

At the primary shortlist size, both methods retain a Brier test oracle in every new-seed task and have the same mean selected regret. Metric-specific outcomes vary: one A-opt task has only $\WorstErrorRecall\%$ error-ranked recall, and validation misses a retained test-error oracle. Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics} gives the task details and full comparator table.

\subsection{Target weighting under the shared risk model}
The earlier synthetic study asks whether target weighting helps when the shared risk model holds. It varies feature dimension, candidate count, source budget, target sample size, and target rank, using 20 seeds for 216 settings. A-opt reduces modeled risk by $4.23\%$ relative to the strongest target-blind effective-rank comparator and by $14.85\%$ relative to MMD. Descriptive 95\% intervals are $[3.86,4.62]\%$ and $[13.99,15.70]\%$, resampling settings after averaging seeds. Here the score is evaluated on modeled linear risk; classification tests its usefulness for shortlisting. A-opt's advantage here and MMD's competitive classification results answer different questions, with no contradiction. These comparisons alone do not identify why their relative performance differs.

A separate source-conditional-shift sweep changes the regression mechanism. Mean normalized prediction regret increases from $0.211$ without shift to $3.143$ at the largest tested shift. This measures realized prediction, not posterior risk: covering target directions and transferring labels across conditional mechanisms are different requirements.

\subsection{Direct selection and available improvement headroom}
PACS and Office-Home test whether the score can directly choose a better source combination. At block budget $K=3$, A-opt improves mean within-unit relative Brier by $0.351\%$ over DPP and $0.273\%$ over MMD. Both fall below the prespecified $2\%$ practical-effect threshold. The paired difference interval crosses zero against DPP and is negative against MMD; detailed values are in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics}. These comparisons hold block count, rather than source sample count, fixed.

The post-hoc exhaustive audit measures headroom: the loss reduction available from the test-best candidate over the reference. Mean headroom over DPP is only $0.781\%$ (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics}), limiting average gains without changing the failed prespecified test. This motivates distinguishing better predictions from less evaluation work. These studies provide context, not independent equal-cost screening replications.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{What makes screening useful?}
Screening should be evaluated within the selection procedure. Final loss determines whether fewer evaluations leave an acceptable choice; omission and validation diagnostics explain quality losses. Timing establishes whether work is saved. The readout-dependent cost reversal is central: a useful shortlist need not be economical when fitting is already cheap.

MMD and domain matching show why detailed geometry needs simple matching baselines. Good global choices need not distinguish individual blocks, although block information can help within fixed domain mixtures. Operating points should reflect the target metric, acceptable loss gap, and measured cost together, not recall or full-ranking correlation alone.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The equal-sample-cost evidence uses two constructions on the same DomainNet images. Target domains share source pools; added seeds and readouts measure within-benchmark sensitivity, not independent-data replication. Differences from PACS/Office-Home include dataset, projection, sample budget, and construction, preventing causal attribution to candidate heterogeneity alone.

The derivation concerns shared linear regression; classification evaluates the score empirically. Test-example resampling conditions on fitted models and validation choices, excluding uncertainty from training or selection. Absolute quality also depends on the candidate family, as the supplementary adapter study illustrates. Image costs add measured components, excluding pool construction and cache serialization, with features profiled once on one hardware setup. Independent equal-cost datasets, controlled validation budgets, and checks for encoder-pretraining overlap remain important.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We evaluated target-aware geometry as a way to reduce supervised data-pool evaluations while retaining a competitive final choice. The analysis explains the design score under shared linear risk and distinguishes candidate omission from validation error. On equal-sample-cost DomainNet combinations, evaluating 50 A-opt or MMD candidates gives lower mean test Brier than evaluating 227 random candidates. Simple moment matching remains competitive. Fewer evaluations save time with cached features; adding measured feature costs preserves the logistic-regression saving but reverses it for ridge. Screening is therefore best assessed through the final loss and the actual work required to obtain that choice, with candidate retention explaining rather than replacing those outcomes.
