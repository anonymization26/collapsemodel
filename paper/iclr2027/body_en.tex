\section{Introduction}
Researchers adapting a predictor to a target domain often have several source-data pools to choose from. The pools may differ in acquisition conditions, image style, or the range of examples they contain. A source combination is useful only in relation to the intended prediction task, so its quality is usually assessed by training a model and evaluating it on target validation data. Repeating this procedure for every combination can be costly even when each individual model is small. The practical problem is deciding which combinations deserve that supervised evaluation.

Pretrained encoders provide a common space in which to inspect these choices. An encoder maps each image to a feature vector; with its parameters frozen, the same extracted features can be reused across source combinations and classifier fits. Learned visual representations such as CLIP and DINOv2 make this setting natural \citep{radford2021,oquab2024}. Stacking feature vectors as rows of a matrix exposes properties that can be measured without fitting a classifier. Sample counts indicate the amount of data, while singular values describe how strongly different feature directions are represented. These measurements offer a possible first step in a search that would otherwise require labels at every comparison.

Effective rank is one way to summarize that directional structure. Ordinary matrix rank counts nonzero singular values, so it can assign the same rank to a well-spread representation and one dominated by a few directions. Effective rank instead normalizes the singular values, takes their entropy, and exponentiates the result \citep{roy2007}. It describes how many equally weighted directions would have the same spectral entropy. RankMe uses this idea to assess self-supervised representations without downstream labels \citep{garrido2023rankme}. For source-pool selection, the corresponding intuition is that a combination covering more directions may offer a richer set of features for learning.

Moving from representation assessment to pool selection introduces two questions. First, how do the directions supplied by different pools combine? Two pools can each have broad spectra while covering much the same part of feature space; another pair can complement one another. Second, which of the resulting directions matter to the target? A combination may have high effective rank because it contains variation rarely encountered at prediction time. For example, if the target occupies a narrow part of feature space, adding variation outside that part can raise a diversity score without improving its coverage. A pooled spectrum describes source variation, whereas prediction also depends on the target distribution and the relation between features and labels.

These questions suggest using geometry to retain alternatives rather than requiring it to choose the final model. A screen can keep several combinations whose features look relevant and let labeled validation resolve the remaining uncertainty. The resulting objective is to preserve useful candidates within a smaller evaluation budget. It differs from maximizing a geometric score for its own sake: a useful shortlist should contain candidates with good target outcomes, even when their exact ordering is hard to predict from features.

We study this approach through a two-stage data-pool screening protocol. The first stage ranks combinations using unlabeled source and target features; the second trains on source labels and chooses within the shortlist using separate target-validation data. Target-weighted A-optimal design favors source combinations that reduce uncertainty along directions common in the target, a connection made explicit by a shared Bayesian linear model. Second-moment matching asks a simpler question: how closely does the source's average directional energy match the target's? Comparing these scores with merged effective rank and other target-blind criteria distinguishes the use of target information from the choice of scoring function.

The experiments evaluate both the retained candidates and the final validation choice. Direct selection on PACS and Office-Home produces modest gains, and exhaustive evaluation finds little room to improve on the baselines within those candidate families. An equal-sample-cost DomainNet study instead shows that target-aware screening can retain most promising combinations while shortening the list sent to supervised evaluation. Additional projection seeds and classifier readouts preserve high average recall, and second-moment matching remains competitive with A-optimal screening. By relating these observations to the construction of effective rank and the assumptions behind target-weighted risk, the paper identifies what geometry contributes to pool screening and what must be decided using task-specific evidence.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Representation geometry and diversity}
RankMe uses effective rank to assess learned representations without downstream labels \citep{garrido2023rankme}. Determinantal point processes favor subsets with complementary directions \citep{kulesza2012}. Both motivate ways to compare source pools before fitting a task-specific model. Their geometric objectives differ, however, from preserving candidates with low target loss: variation may be redundant for a particular task, and directions important to that task may occupy only part of the representation. We examine merged effective rank and a specified subspace-DPP implementation as target-blind comparators.

\subsection{Optimal design and distribution matching}
A-optimal design measures uncertainty through the trace of a covariance matrix, with applications to active linear regression \citep{fontaine2021}. Weighting that covariance by a target feature moment directs the criterion toward predictions on the intended population. Distribution matching offers a different approach. MMD compares mean embeddings of source and target distributions \citep{gretton2012}; a homogeneous quadratic kernel makes this comparison depend on their second moments. Moment alignment has also been used to learn representations for multi-source domain adaptation \citep{peng2019}. Here the encoder stays fixed, allowing us to compare uncertainty reduction and moment matching as scoring rules on the same candidate combinations.

\subsection{Selection under a computation budget}
Compute-constrained data selection accounts for the work needed to select data as well as the subsequent training \citep{yin2025}. Pool screening introduces an intermediate decision: how many combinations should receive supervised evaluation? A broad shortlist may be easy to construct and preserve good candidates, while still requiring substantial validation work. Our protocol measures retention at a declared shortlist size and separates it from the quality of the final selected model. This provides the decision-level evidence needed before measuring the total cost of a deployment.

\section{Geometry-Guided Screening}
\label{sec:problem}
\label{sec:theory}
\subsection{Problem and two-stage procedure}
Let $H_i\in\R^{n_i\times d}$ contain the features of source pool $i$, obtained with a fixed encoder and a shared, label-independent preprocessing map. Write $G_i=H_i^\top H_i$. A feasible combination $S$ belongs to a finite family $\cC$, with $G_S=\sum_{i\in S}G_i$ and $n_S=\sum_{i\in S}n_i$. An unlabeled target-selection sample estimates the uncentered second moment $C_T=\E_T[hh^\top]$.

The screen ranks $\cC$ and retains its first $s$ combinations as $\cQ_s$. We then train predictors on their source labels and use a separate target-validation set to choose $\widehat S\in\cQ_s$. Target-test data evaluate this frozen choice and the candidates retained. The screening stage, denoted U0, sees source and target-selection features but no labels or validation/test data. The supervised stage, U2, reads source labels and target-validation data. Target-blind rules use only source information. This separation lets the screen exploit reusable features while reserving task-specific evidence for the final choice.

Here a pool is a fixed block of examples, and a candidate is a feasible union of such blocks. We select among these unions rather than choose individual images or retrain the encoder. This distinction fixes what a screening score is allowed to change. Each retained combination receives the same prescribed classifier and validation procedure, so differences in its outcome come from the source examples supplied to that procedure. In the equal-cost study, all combinations also contain the same number of examples.

\subsection{Constructing effective rank from pooled features}
The singular value decomposition $H=U\Sigma V^\top$ separates feature directions from their strengths. The columns of $V$ give directions in the encoder's coordinate space, and the diagonal entries of $\Sigma$ measure the magnitude of the feature matrix along those directions. Since $H^\top H=V\Sigma^\top\Sigma V^\top$, the Gram matrix contains the information needed to recover them. It also gives a convenient way to combine pools without repeatedly stacking their feature rows.

For fixed nonnegative weights $w_i$, vertically stacking source features gives
\begin{equation}
 H_S=\begin{bmatrix}\sqrt{w_{i_1}}H_{i_1}\\ \vdots\\\sqrt{w_{i_k}}H_{i_k}\end{bmatrix},
 \qquad H_S^\top H_S=\sum_{i\in S}w_iG_i.
 \label{eq:gram}
\end{equation}
The Gram sum therefore determines the merged singular values in the shared feature coordinates. Keeping these operators preserves both the strength of each direction and its alignment across pools. Re-centering or re-fitting a projection for each combination would change those coordinates and require a different calculation.

We construct the effective rank by normalizing the singular values into a probability vector and exponentiating its Shannon entropy \citep{roy2007}:
\begin{equation}
 \reff(H)=\exp\!\left(-\sum_j p_j\log p_j\right),\qquad
 p_j=\frac{\sigma_j(H)}{\sum_k\sigma_k(H)},
 \label{eq:rank}
\end{equation}
The convention $0\log0=0$ handles zero singular values for a nonzero matrix. If $\rho$ singular values are positive, the entropy lies between $0$ and $\log\rho$, so $1\leq\reff(H)\leq\rho$. A rank-one matrix attains the lower endpoint; equal positive singular values attain the upper endpoint. For two positive singular values with ratio $9:1$, ordinary rank is two, but effective rank is about $1.38$. Equal singular values instead give effective rank two. Thus the score distinguishes concentrated from balanced variation even when ordinary rank is unchanged.

In implementation, we first sum the source Grams, compute their nonnegative eigenvalues, and take square roots to obtain the singular values used in Equation~\eqref{eq:rank}. Normalizing the Gram eigenvalues directly would give a different score, with more weight on large directions. A common scalar rescaling cancels in the normalized singular values. In particular, repeating an entire feature matrix multiplies every singular value by the same factor and leaves effective rank unchanged. The statistic measures spectral shape, not the amount of labeled evidence available for training. Numerical tolerances are specified in Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}.

The direction information in the Gram sum is important before this spectral summary is taken. Keeping only each pool's spectrum and a list of subspace overlaps can already lose information needed to calculate the merged effective rank.

\begin{proposition}[Directional assignment matters]
There exist row-normalized pairs $(A,B_1)$ and $(A,B_2)$ with identical marginal singular spectra and identical principal angles between their row spaces, but different effective ranks after vertical concatenation.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Take $A$ with four rows $e_1^\top$ and one row $e_2^\top$. Let $B_1$ have four rows $e_1^\top$ and one $e_3^\top$, and let $B_2$ have one row $e_1^\top$ and four $e_3^\top$. All three nonzero singular spectra are $(2,1)$; both row-space pairs have principal angles $(0,\pi/2)$. The merged Gram spectra are $(8,1,1)$ and $(5,4,1)$, giving effective ranks of approximately $2.6260$ and $2.8495$.
\end{proof}
The two merges assign different amounts of energy to their shared direction. This explains why our screening rules use Gram operators rather than marginal spectra alone. Appendix~\ref{app:proofs} also gives an exact local spectral formula when the operators admit compatible two-direction blocks.

\subsection{Target weighting and moment matching}
To connect geometry to prediction, consider scalar Bayesian linear regression with $\theta\sim N(0,P_0)$, $P_0\succ0$, and $y=h^\top\theta+\epsilon$, where independent noise satisfies $\epsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^2)$ and $\sigma^2>0$. Sources and target share $\theta$ and the noise model; target covariates reveal no further information about $\theta$. Conditional on the source design, the posterior covariance and posterior-mean target risk are
\begin{equation}
 P_S=\left(P_0^{-1}+\sigma^{-2}G_S\right)^{-1},\qquad
 R_T(S)=\sigma^2+\tr(C_TP_S).
 \label{eq:risk}
\end{equation}
The expectation averages posterior uncertainty and a new target observation. The derivation is recalled in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

Equation~\eqref{eq:risk} motivates the score $f_A(S)=\tr(\widehat C_TP_S)$, minimized with fixed hyperparameters. Source observations reduce uncertainty in the directions they cover, and the target moment weights those reductions by how often the directions occur at prediction time. Thus a pool can be valuable even without high overall diversity if it covers directions that remain uncertain and matter to the target. Adding a positive-semidefinite source Gram cannot increase this score.

For the experimental choice $P_0^{-1}=I$ and $\sigma^2=1$, write $G_S=U\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_j)U^\top$, including zero eigenvalues. Then
\begin{equation}
 f_A(S)=\sum_j\frac{u_j^\top\widehat C_Tu_j}{1+\lambda_j}.
 \label{eq:target-directions}
\end{equation}
The denominator reflects source coverage of direction $u_j$, while the numerator measures its importance to the target. Two combinations with the same eigenvalues can therefore receive different scores if their directions align differently with the target. Effective rank treats their spectra identically; target weighting distinguishes where their coverage is useful.

Second-moment matching instead minimizes
\begin{equation}
 f_M(S)=\norm{G_S/n_S-\widehat C_T}_F^2.
 \label{eq:mmd}
\end{equation}
This is empirical squared MMD with kernel $k(h,h')=(h^\top h')^2$, including diagonal terms. It brings the source's average directional energy closer to the target's rather than weighting a posterior uncertainty matrix. Both criteria read the same features, so their comparison tests whether inverse-covariance weighting adds value beyond direct moment matching.

The shared conditional model supplies the risk interpretation of A-opt. Its assumption is substantive: if all features equal $1$, source pools labeled constantly $+1$ and $-1$ produce predictors whose risk ordering reverses between two target worlds with opposite constant labels. The feature geometry is unchanged. In classification, we therefore evaluate the scores using trained ridge-softmax and logistic-regression readouts rather than identify Equation~\eqref{eq:risk} with their losses.

\subsection{Separating retention from final selection}
Let $\ell(S)$ denote target risk for a fixed trained predictor and $v(S)$ its validation loss. If $|v(S)-\ell(S)|\leq\varepsilon$ throughout $\cQ_s$, then the validation minimizer satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \ell(\widehat S)-\min_{S\in\cC}\ell(S)
 \leq
 \underbrace{\min_{S\in\cQ_s}\ell(S)-\min_{S\in\cC}\ell(S)}_{\text{shortlist omission}}
 +2\varepsilon.
 \label{eq:decomposition}
\end{equation}
The proof appears in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. The first term asks whether screening discarded the best available choice; the second concerns choosing within the retained set. A shortlist that preserves an oracle can still lead to an inferior final model if validation ranks its members differently. We audit their finite-test counterparts and also measure recall of the test top ten, which asks whether several good alternatives survive screening. The deviation condition is used to explain these two error sources, not estimated as a population guarantee in our experiments.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
Controlled synthetic tasks test the risk model. PACS and Office-Home \citep{li2017,venkateswara2017} then evaluate direct selection against a prespecified practical-effect threshold. An exhaustive headroom analysis follows those outcomes. The subsequent DomainNet study fixes sample cost and evaluates shortlists; its configuration was frozen before feature extraction and outcome evaluation. Test-sample resampling and the later projection/readout study are exploratory follow-ups. Appendix~\ref{app:protocol} records their configurations and result locations.

From the official cleaned DomainNet data \citep{peng2019}, we select 100 class names by a fixed hash rule. Each of six domains contributes disjoint sets of 1,536 target-selection images, 1,024 target-validation images, and 3,072 anchor-pool images from official training data, plus 2,048 official test images. The manifest contains 46,080 images. Class metadata define the benchmark vocabulary but are hidden from screening.

CLIP ViT-B/32 \citep{radford2021} serves as a frozen construction anchor. Within each domain, its first principal-component scores define low, middle, and high strata, each supplying a block of 512 images. For a given target, we exclude its own blocks and enumerate $\binom{15}{3}=455$ combinations, each with 1,536 source images. ResNet-50 and DINOv2-B/14 provide evaluation features \citep{he2016,oquab2024}. All rules share row normalization, a fixed Rademacher projection to 128 dimensions, and post-projection row normalization. Holding sample count constant removes a direct source of training-cost differences between candidates.

Target A-opt uses $P_0^{-1}=I$ and $\sigma^2=1$. Comparators are second-moment MMD, target energy $\tr(\widehat C_TG_S/n_S)$, Bayesian D-opt, merged effective rank, subspace DPP, domain balance, and 20 seeded random rankings. They score the same candidate family. Domain balance additionally reads source-domain identifiers. Kernel definitions and tie rules appear in Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}.

The screen produces nested shortlists of sizes $10,25,50,100,227$, with $227$ designated primary. In the original protocol, U2 independently evaluates each method's primary shortlist using ridge regression with regularization $1$, no intercept, and a fixed softmax temperature. Validation Brier selects the final combination, breaking ties lexicographically. Smaller lists reuse that method's nested evaluations; there is no cross-method cache. The test stage verifies upstream hashes before evaluating every combination to establish an exhaustive audit reference.

The follow-up adds four projection seeds and a multinomial logistic readout with fixed regularization. It keeps the candidate members and data roles unchanged. Screen, validation, and test remain separate processes, but the follow-up reuses each combination's fitted model across methods to audit their shortlists. Its runtime is therefore the cost of a cached research audit rather than separate deployments. Readout settings and provenance checks are detailed in Appendix~\ref{app:protocol}.

We measure how many test top-ten candidates survive screening and how far the validation choice lies from the empirical optimum. Writing test Brier as $b(S)$ and its exhaustive minimizer as $S^*$, these quantities are
\begin{equation}
 \mathrm{Recall}_{10}(\cQ_s)=\frac{|\cQ_s\cap\mathrm{Top}_{10}(b)|}{10},\qquad
 \mathrm{Regret}(\widehat S)=\frac{b(\widehat S)-b(S^*)}{|b(S^*)|}.
 \label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
The original acceptance criteria require at least $50\%$ removal, mean recall of at least $90\%$, and mean normalized regret at most $5\%$. Brier is primary. Follow-up NLL and classification-error audits use their corresponding validation metric to select candidates, with the metric fixed before test access.

We average encoder results within each dataset--target-domain unit and random rankings within each task. PACS/Office-Home contain eight such units and DomainNet six. Follow-up seeds are also averaged within domains. Original bootstrap intervals resample target units; the test-sample analysis fixes trained models and resamples examples within each target. Seeds, encoders, and overlapping combinations are repeated measurements, not additional independent datasets.

\section{Empirical Findings}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Target weighting under the shared risk model}
The synthetic study varies feature dimension, candidate count, source budget, target sample size, and target rank, using 20 generation seeds for 216 design settings. Under the shared conditional model, A-opt reduces modeled risk by $4.23\%$ relative to the strongest target-blind effective-rank comparator and by $14.85\%$ relative to MMD. The respective descriptive 95\% intervals are $[3.86,4.62]\%$ and $[13.99,15.70]\%$, resampling design settings after averaging seeds. These comparisons show the benefit of weighting uncertainty by the target when the model that motivates this operation holds.

A separate source-conditional-shift sweep changes the regression mechanism. Its mean normalized prediction regret increases from $0.211$ without shift to $3.143$ at the largest tested shift. This endpoint measures realized prediction rather than posterior risk. Together, the two studies distinguish the advantage of covering target-relevant directions from the effect of transferring labels across different conditional mechanisms.

\subsection{Direct selection and available improvement headroom}
PACS and Office-Home test whether the score can directly choose a better source combination. At block budget $K=3$, A-opt improves mean within-unit relative Brier by $0.351\%$ over DPP and $0.273\%$ over MMD. Both fall below the prespecified $2\%$ practical-effect threshold. The paired difference interval crosses zero against DPP and is negative against MMD; detailed values are in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics}. These comparisons hold block count, rather than source sample count, fixed.

To understand the scale of the available gains, the post-hoc audit evaluates every feasible combination. Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics} shows that even the test oracle offers only $0.781\%$ mean headroom over DPP. Most target units therefore leave little room for a different selector to improve the average loss substantially. This observation motivates examining candidate retention separately: geometry may help narrow the search even when the final choices have similar loss.

Table~\ref{tab:primary} compares the original DomainNet shortlists. A-opt retains nearly all test top-ten candidates while discarding approximately half the combinations. Every primary shortlist contains a test Brier oracle; the remaining selected regret comes from validation choosing another member. This is the distinction captured by Equation~\eqref{eq:decomposition}: successful screening leaves a separate task for validation.

The post-hoc loss-span diagnostic in Figure~\ref{fig:diagnostics} puts the original regret criterion in context. Every candidate's relative Brier regret lies below the $5\%$ threshold, so that threshold cannot distinguish successful screens in this experiment. Recall provides the more informative comparison, but it raises another question: how sensitive is the test top ten to the sampled test examples?

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{What makes screening useful?}
The results suggest evaluating screening in terms of the decision it enables. When the candidate family offers little improvement headroom, a small selected loss difference says little about the quality of a ranking. When candidates differ more, retaining several promising alternatives gives validation room to make a task-specific choice. A-opt and MMD both do this well on the audited DomainNet construction; their similar results make moment matching an important baseline for more elaborate geometric scores.

The natural operating point depends on the target metric, the acceptable loss gap, and the cost of supervised evaluation. Shortlist size controls how many candidates proceed, but total savings also depend on scoring, feature preparation, and validation costs. Separately, the sketch experiments in Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics} show that valid score intervals can remain too wide to resolve choices at a useful byte budget. For a compressed score to support screening, it must still distinguish candidates at the intended shortlist budget.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
The equal-sample-cost evidence comes from one DomainNet construction. Its target domains share source pools, and the added seeds and readouts reuse the same images. They measure sensitivity within this benchmark rather than replication on independent data. Changes from PACS/Office-Home also include the dataset, projection, sample budget, and candidate construction, so they do not isolate candidate heterogeneity as a cause.

The Bayesian derivation concerns shared linear regression; the classification results establish the usefulness of its score empirically. Test-example resampling conditions on fitted models and validation choices. It does not measure uncertainty from training or validation selection. Absolute task quality also depends on the candidate family, as the supplementary adapter study illustrates. Finally, we have not measured matched end-to-end savings or ruled out overlap with encoder pretraining data. Independent equal-cost datasets, controlled validation budgets, and matched timing comparisons would address these remaining questions.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
We developed a two-stage protocol that uses feature geometry to shortlist source-data combinations before supervised validation. The analysis explains how target weighting connects source coverage to risk under a shared linear model and separates screening omissions from final selection errors. Experiments show high candidate retention on equal-cost DomainNet combinations across projection seeds and readouts, with second-moment matching as effective as A-optimal screening at the primary operating point. Direct-selection results on PACS and Office-Home show that available improvement headroom also matters. Together, these findings support using target-aware geometry to narrow a search whose final decision is made with task-specific evidence. Measuring that decision's quality alongside the full evaluation cost is the next step toward choosing useful screening budgets.
